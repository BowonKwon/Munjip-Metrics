한국어

Reading edition

Size

Collections, works and characters. Characters are counted separately for the main text and for notes (original notes, collation and marginal notes).

Works per collection

637,203 works in 1,249 collections; the median collection has 339. The largest are the Songja daejeon (9,434), the Yeoyudang jeonseo (8,100) and the Myeonu jip (5,688). 95.7% of all works are by the collection's own author.

Characters per collection

180.3 million characters of text and 2.6 million in notes (1.4%); the median collection has about 80,000. The Yeoyudang jeonseo is the longest at 4.1 million, followed by the Songja daejeon (3.7 million) and the Myeonu jip (2.9 million).

Collections by century

By the author's birth century the collections cluster in the 16th (382), 17th (353) and 18th (272); only 48 belong to authors born before 1400. The 586 supplement volumes fill gaps left by the first series, including 48 authors born in the 19th century.

Characters per work

The median work is 56 characters (one seven-syllable regulated verse) up to the 16th century, then 75 in the 17th, 118 in the 18th and 157 in the 19th — the same direction as the falling share of poetry.

Share of characters in notes

Notes make up 1.4% of all characters, staying between 1.0% and 1.8% in every century except the 9th (Choe Chi-won, 5.7%).

Share of own works

95.7% of works are by the author; the rest are prefaces, postscripts, biographies, elegies and appendices by others. In three collections no writer tag matches the author at all — possibly a tagging convention, flagged for sample checking.

Fascicles and works per fascicle

Of the 851 collections whose introduction states 'n fascicles, n volumes', 691 (81%) have exactly n '卷n' headings in the table of contents; most of the other 160 number continuations, separate collections or appendices on their own.

Genre composition

Counts the genre classification the ITKC attached to every work (7 major, 28 minor classes). Headline shares use the author's own works only (writer = author).

Share of poetry

62.0% of own works are poems. By century the share falls from 88% (14th–15th) to 75% (16th), 62% (17th), 51% (18th) and 42% (19th). By characters poetry is only 18.8%: many works, few characters. The direction matches Kwak (2026): poetry largest, declining.

Seven-class genre mix

Poetry 62.0%, letters 13.4%, miscellany 9.1%, court documents 5.6%, biographies 3.7%, elegies 3.2%, prefaces 3.0%. By characters the order changes: miscellany 25.2%, poetry 18.8%, letters 17.8%, biographies 16.9%. Among minor classes poems (373,534), letters (81,423) and memorials (26,369) lead.

Share of letters

Letters are 13.4% of own works: around 1% before the 16th century, then 5.7% (16th), 15.0% (17th), 15.1% (18th) and 30.2% (19th) — the rising trend Kwak (2026) reports.

Share of court documents

Court documents (memorials, examination answers, edicts) are 5.6% of own works. Apart from the 9th century (Choe Chi-won, 42%), the share peaks in the 16th (5.8%) and 17th (7.7%) centuries and falls to 2.4% in the 19th.

Share of elegies, epitaphs and biographies

Works commemorating others (elegies, epitaphs, biographies) are 6.4% of own works, rising from about 2% in the 14th–15th centuries to 6.3% (17th), 8.3% (18th) and 9.8% (19th).

Share of prefaces and postscripts

Prefaces and postscripts are 3.0% of own works: under 2% through the 16th century, 4.6% in the 18th and 5.6% in the 19th.

Residual 'other' share

6.4% of own works sit in an 'other' minor class, between 2.8% (15th century) and 9.4% (18th), except the 9th century at 30%. The largest block is 'other' within miscellany (26,493 works).

Characters per poem

The median poem has 56 characters in every century — the length of one seven-syllable regulated verse. Quartiles lie at 28 (quatrains) and 60–110; the mean (64–96) exceeds the median because of cycles and long poems.

Relationship genres

Addressees of letters and subjects of epitaphs and biographies, parsed from titles as strings. Version 1.0 does not identify persons: the same person called by courtesy name, pen name or office counts as different strings.

Letter addressees and concentration

In the 895 collections with letters, 93.7% of the 81,842 letters name an addressee in the title, giving 34,980 addressee strings. A collection's median is 14 distinct addressees, with the top three taking 41% of its letters. The Ganjae jip (1,290), Myeonu jip (1,033) and Songja daejeon (801) have the most.

Subjects of epitaphs and biographies

880 collections contain 19,789 epitaphs and biographies by the author; a subject string was parsed from 94.8% (17,972 distinct). The Songsa jip (968), Songja daejeon (576) and Myeonam jip (428) have the most.

Other writers

1,120 collections carry 27,709 works by writers other than the author; summed over collections that is 17,675 writers, a median of 8 per collection. The Seongjae jip (173), Hanju jip (143) and Cheogam jip (138) have the most.

Authors and publication

Author dates, linkage to the civil-examination roster, the lag between death and publication, and translation and punctuation progress.

Date completeness and lifespan

Of 1,248 authors, 1,240 have a birth year and 1,231 a death year. Median lifespan is 65.5 years, ranging from 57.5 (15th century) to 70 (18th). Of 1,072 authors matched to the AKS person database by name and birth year, 1,030 (96.1%) agree on the death year.

Examination linkage and age at passing

48.9% of the 1,248 authors (610) link to a passer in the civil-examination roster: 82% of those born in the 15th century, 62% (16th), 52% (17th), 29% (18th), 22% (19th). The decline reflects more collections by non-passers (rural scholars, monks, women); it is not a pass rate. Median age at passing is 30 (27 in the 15th century, 35 in the 18th).

Publication lag

For the 1,026 collections with a printing year, the median lag from the author's death is 66 years (quartiles 24–177). It shortens by birth century: 175 years (15th), 128 (16th), 59 (17th), 39 (18th), 15 (19th).

Linked-translation progress

16.6% of works have a linked translation; 1,105 collections have none (585 of the 586 supplement volumes). Only 143 first-series collections carry a link, fewer than the 372 Choi (2020) counted as translated — linkage measures database work, not translation.

Collation and punctuation progress

6.5% of works have a linked collated and punctuated edition, concentrated in 58 collections. The share by century ranges from 2% to 26% because the program's target collections are unevenly spread.
